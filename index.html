Context: the problem

🎯 Why this matters

Before the method: what CDK2 is, why a drug that hits it is worth having, and why hitting only it is the hard part.

CDK2 and the cell cycle

A dividing cell moves through four phases, and it decides whether to commit at the step from growth into copying its DNA. CDK2, cyclin-dependent kinase 2, is one of the enzymes that drives that step.

In many tumours the cell keeps taking that step when it should stop, so blocking CDK2 is worth trying. Interest sharpened once CDK4/6 inhibitors reached the clinic and tumours escaped them by routes running through CDK2.

The problem: kinases all look alike inside

Almost every kinase inhibitor works the same way: it plugs the pocket where the enzyme would otherwise bind ATP. Humans have on the order of five hundred kinases, and that pocket is the part of them that is most alike.

Those relatives have their own jobs, so hitting them is not free. Unintended kinase activity is a known source of toxicity, of dose limits that keep a drug below a useful concentration, and of compounds that fail late and expensively.

Potency is not the goal. Selectivity is.

"Binds CDK2 strongly" is easy to ask a computer for and easy to measure. What a project actually needs is binds CDK2 strongly and the neighbouring kinases weakly, which is a statement about where the molecule must not go. An objective that only rewards affinity cannot express that, because nothing in it ever counts a bad outcome.

What a project like this is trying to produce

Nothing here is a drug, and nothing here has been near a laboratory. The chemical space a medicinal chemist could plausibly draw is far larger than the number of molecules anyone can afford to make and assay. So the real product of a computational stage is a shortlist: a few candidate molecules that are worth the cost of synthesis and testing, chosen out of tens of thousands that are not.

Every filter applied before that shortlist changes which molecules survive to be looked at. The question this work asks is narrow and deliberately so: if the generator is told about off-target binding, does it keep different molecules? Not "are they better drugs", which needs a laboratory. Whether the preference shows up at all, and what it costs, is what a computer can settle.

What ALGen is, and what this work adds

ALGen is not new here. It is an existing machine-learning framework, built and published by the group this thesis was carried out in, and it is the starting point rather than the contribution. In outline: a neural network that has learned to read and write molecules as text proposes new ones; physics-based docking scores how well each proposal fits a protein pocket; the best-scoring molecules are added back into the network's training set, so the next round is generated from better chemistry than the last. Generate, score, keep, repeat: an active-learning loop with a physics model in it.

As published, that loop optimised for one protein. It rewarded binding CDK2 and had no way to express, or even to see, that a molecule also bound something else. A later extension handled several proteins at once, but by rewarding binding at all of them. That is multi-target affinity, a different objective from selectivity and in places the opposite one.

Inherited, unchanged

The generative model, the chemistry filters, the active-learning loop, the CDK2 docking setup, and the run configuration. All fixed, so that anything observed downstream is attributable.

Added in this work

A validated antitarget panel of related kinases with docking grids and a common scale, because the measurement had to be built before it could be optimised, and one new antitarget term in the score that decides which molecules the loop keeps.

The experiment is then as clean as it can be made: run the loop twice from the same starting chemistry, with that one term switched on in one run and off in the other, and compare what each run selected. Two runs, one difference.

How the off-target signal was built ↓

Method: the measurement

🧪 Building the selectivity signal

Most of the work here was not the generative experiment. It was making off-target liability measurable at all.

Docking, throughout, means placing a molecule in a protein's binding pocket in silico and scoring how well it fits. A Glide score is that estimate; more negative means stronger predicted binding. Nothing on this page is a measured affinity.

Why CDK2 selectivity is hard

Protein kinases share a conserved catalytic fold and a highly similar ATP-binding cleft. A compound optimised only for CDK2 affinity will often also hit its close relatives, and off-target activity at those kinases interferes with their own cellular roles.

So the design objective is not "bind CDK2 hard". It is bind CDK2 hard while binding a defined set of related kinases weakly. That set is the antitarget panel.

Technical details: why CDK2 selectivity is hard
  • Type-I inhibitors, the class relevant here, compete with ATP at the site whose geometry is the most conserved across the whole family.
  • The measured selectivity margin is Δp = pCDK2 − maxj pAT,j, with p = −log₁₀X. It is panel-dependent: it measures activity relative to the strongest measured antitarget, not an absolute property of the molecule.
  • Docking discriminates actives from inactives, but is much weaker at resolving small potency differences among ligands that are already active, and its score distributions depend on the receptor and on the grid. Both facts drive design decisions below: the first in reliable ≠ useful, the second in one common scale.
How the panel, the reference chemistry and the docking grids were built: three subsections

Choosing the ten antitargets

Compounds reported potent against CDK2 (≤ 1 µM) were pulled from ChEMBL together with every available measurement against other human kinases. That gave 1,535 CDK2-active compounds with usable profiles.

Candidate kinases were ranked by how many of those compounds had been measured against them, and the top ten became the panel:

CDK1 · GSK3B · CDK9 · CDK5 · CDK7 · CDK6 · VEGFR2 · DYRK1A · Aurora B · PIM1

Read that ranking for what it is

It mostly reflects assay coverage, which kinases people happened to test. It is a statement about the literature, not directly about biology. Sequence relationships were used afterwards as context, never as the criterion. The panel was fixed at this point, before any receptor selection or docking calibration.

Protocol and validation: the reference chemistry
  • Standardisation: RDKit: largest organic fragment retained, formal charges neutralised, canonicalised. Identity is the connectivity block of the InChIKey.
  • Replicates: median within activity type. IC₅₀, Ki and Kd are not numerically pooled; precedence IC₅₀ > Ki > Kd. Contradictory evidence is flagged ambiguous, not averaged.
  • After curation: 30,744 compounds, 44,604 compound–kinase pairs.
  • Applicability domain: MW 280.851–609.193 Da, deliberately narrower than the 280.85–700 Da filter used during generation.
  • Classes: active at p ≥ 7, inactive at p < 5, or when a censored measurement places activity beyond the weak-binding threshold. Intermediate measurements are treated as weak, not decisive.
  • How S5 was built: exclude any candidate with a recorded measurement against any of the eleven panel kinases, then match to CDK2-active chemistry within MW ± 25 Da, cLogP ± 1.0, HBA ± 2, HBD ± 1, rotatable bonds ± 2, identical formal charge. Each decoy used once. 43,546 candidates → 9,259 matches → deterministic cap at 2,000.
  • Complete receptor-panel profiles exist for S1, S2 and S5; S3 and S4 give CDK2-specific calibration only.
  • Scaffold grouping throughout uses Bemis–Murcko scaffolds (RDKit).

From crystal structures to docking grids

Every panel kinase was rebuilt from experimental structures, aligned into one frame, and reduced to the smallest set of conformations the data actually supports, with one exception, Aurora B, which is benchmark-only and carries a single structure (4AF3_A) forward directly.

1,005 PDB structures → 12 production docking grids over 9 kinases.

Grids outnumber kinases

CDK2, CDK1 and CDK7 each contribute two, because their pocket geometry supported a validated conformational split. "Ten antitarget grids" and "eight antitarget kinases" are both correct.

Protocol and validation: from crystal structures to docking grids

Collection

Structures were identified in the PDB by UniProt accession and screened on method, resolution ≤ 3.2 Å, chain length and title exclusions → 1,005 unique entries / 1,406 kinase-chain records. Chains were extracted from mmCIF with Gemmi; crystallographic altloc records were kept as separate conformers rather than collapsed.

Preparation

Schrödinger Protein Preparation Wizard (2025-2): hydrogens, protonation at pH ≈ 7.4, Prime rebuild of missing side chains and loops, restrained S-OPLS minimisation.

Pocket completeness

P-loop, catalytic β3 lysine, HRD, DFG and hinge were located in each kinase's UniProt reference sequence and mapped through mmCIF numbering; structures missing any expected pocket residue were excluded. KLIFS annotations were used to retain DFG-in and to exclude annotated allosteric ligands; structures with no KLIFS record were left unassessed rather than assumed clean.

Alignment

Common frame: CDK2 6Q4G_A_altA (KLIFS 11214). Of 1,729 structures entering, 1,420 use direct KLIFS correspondence and 309 within-kinase transfer. Least-squares Kabsch fit of Cα over 11 conserved anchors: β3 Lys (17), gatekeeper (45), hinge (46–48), HRD (68–70), DFG (81–83), requiring ≥ 8 anchors including ≥ 2 per lobe; seven structures fail, leaving 1,722 aligned. Maximum internal-distance change after alignment is < 0.002 Å.

Frame sensitivity

Six alternative CDK2 references (1FIN_A, 3QHW_A, 4I3Z_A, 7ACK_A_altA, 4GCJ_A_altA, 8FP5_A_altA) were chosen to span αC state, nucleotide occupancy, ligand size and activation state rather than resolution. Repeating the whole stratification in all seven frames recovers the same discrete-state conclusions for CDK1, CDK2 and CDK7, although the exact number of CDK2 strata varies between frames (2 to 4).

Conformational stratification

Each structure was reduced to x = (Rcore, Rpocket, Dmax), robustly scaled within kinase by median and IQR. Kinases with fewer than 20 usable structures are not tested. Ward clustering, k = 2 … min(4, ⌊n/5⌋), nmin = max(5, 0.02n), with k chosen by maximum silhouette.

Two gates, both required: the silhouette must exceed the maximum obtained from 20 structureless Gaussian surrogates with matched mean and covariance; and the mean adjusted Rand index over 25 independent 80 % subsamples must exceed 0.6. A permutation null shifts the thresholds but does not change one decision.

Reliable ≠ useful: the two-question test

A grid that separates known binders from known non-binders is reliable. That is not the same as useful. A receptor earns a place in the objective only if its score improves prediction of measured selectivity on chemical families held out from the fit.

Per-kinase docking quality does not predict a receptor's contribution to the objective

CDK9 has the best grid in the panel (ROC AUC 0.890). It is also the only receptor whose inclusion measurably improves selectivity prediction. It was still held out: adding it degraded worst-case robustness on unseen chemical families.

CDK6 has the third-best grid discrimination (0.827) and actively degrades selectivity prediction.

The two questions were therefore tested separately, and panel membership rests on the second.

Protocol and validation: the two-question test

Question 1: is the grid reliable?

  • The enrichment set was assembled independently of S1–S5: measured actives (p ≥ 7) and inactives (p ≤ 5) at that kinase, capped at 400 per class, docked against that kinase's own grid or grids. Scores were reduced across representatives first, so the figures below are kinase-level.
  • Three metrics, because they answer different questions: ROC AUC (the whole ranking), EF1 % (the top percentile only) and BEDROC α = 20 (exponential early weighting). Only AUC enters the weighting.

  • The negative class here is measured inactivity, not S5. S5 has no measurements against any panel kinase and is used only as the normalisation anchor, never as a validated inactive class.

Why this decision?: the two-question test

Question 2: is the receptor useful?

  • The target was measured S1 (selective) vs S2 (promiscuous), on compounds with a complete receptor profile. 254 Bemis–Murcko scaffolds → 5 scaffold-disjoint folds × 3 seeds = 15 held-out tests per candidate subset, against a permutation null of shuffled labels.
  • Subsets were compared on worst fold, not mean. An objective deployed inside a generative loop meets chemistry it has not seen, so behaviour on the least favourable held-out family is the relevant guarantee.
  • A = {DYRK1A, GSK3B, PIM1} attains the highest worst-fold selectivity gain of any subset examined (+0.093). Adding CDK9 improves the mean but degrades worst-fold, and was rejected.
  • Reported rather than smoothed over: selecting on worst fold deliberately sacrifices mean performance; and the deployed composite attains worst-fold enrichment 0.93, marginally below random on one of fifteen folds, with mean 1.49. That is the best of every objective tested on both figures, but it is not a guarantee on unseen chemistry.

The CDK2 docking score is an artefact feature

Unconstrained it looks informative (0.559 against a null of 0.438); forced to act in the physically correct direction it collapses to 0.391, below its own null, permutation P = 0.885. Its entire apparent contribution came from the inverted relation "weaker CDK2 binding predicts selectivity", which is real in the data and useless as an objective.

  • The antitarget block behaves oppositely: constraining it costs 0.070, constraining the full block costs 0.008: it was already being used in the physically correct direction. The antitarget increment over CDK2 + physicochemistry, constrained, is +0.052 (0.673 → 0.725). Extending from eight antitargets to ten gives 0.721: VEGFR2 and Aurora B add nothing.
  • Within the block: CDK5 and CDK6 measurably degrade discrimination (−0.011 and −0.009 AUC, intervals excluding zero), reliable as measurements, carrying information that runs against the label. CDK1 and CDK7 have no detectable effect. CDK9 alone improves it (+0.023, interval excluding zero), and was held out on worst-fold robustness.

Putting every receptor on one scale

A docking score of −8 at one receptor does not mean the same thing as −8 at another. Score distributions depend on the receptor structure and the grid, so raw values cannot be compared or combined across a panel.

Each receptor gets its own ruler

Its scores are expressed relative to its own baseline: the distribution obtained from compounds with no recorded measurement against any panel kinase, the S5 set from the reference chemistry above. After calibration, a score at DYRK1A and a score at CDK2 mean the same thing, and the panel can be combined.

Technical details: putting every receptor on one scale
  • zk = (mk − sk) / σk, with σk = 1.4826 · MADk and mk the median S5 score at receptor k. Larger z means stronger predicted binding relative to that receptor's own baseline. Median and MAD rather than mean and SD, because they are robust to the tail of the decoy distribution.
  • Deployed anchors. CDK2 m = −7.090, σ = 1.118 · DYRK1A m = −7.281, σ = 1.008, w = 0.498 · GSK3B m = −6.474, σ = 0.911, w = 0.271 · PIM1 m = −6.078, σ = 1.153, w = 0.685.
  • Reduction is keyed on molecule and prepared state, not molecule alone. Minimum Glide SP over five poses per prepared state and grid; then the minimum across grids for multi-representative kinases, preserving prepared-state identity.
  • Why that matters: 1,936 of 2,596 reference molecules (74.6 %) yield more than one prepared state, and for 812 of the 2,521 scored at all twelve production receptors (32.2 %) the best-scoring state is not the same state at every receptor. Reducing on the molecule alone would combine a CDK2 score from one physical state with antitarget scores from another, reporting a selectivity that no single prepared structure possesses.
  • States lacking any required kinase-level score are excluded, never imputed.

Method: the objective

🔁 How selection works

Building the selectivity signal built a measurement of predicted off-target liability. This section is where that measurement enters the generator, and just as importantly where it does not.

The ALGen loop

ALGen runs two nested levels. Inside an outer block, repeated inner loops fit the molecular model to the current task-specific corpus, generate proposals, and filter them chemically, with no protein involved at any point. The survivors then enter a structure-based docking and selection stage. The molecules it keeps are added to the corpus, and the next block starts from there.

Docking guides selection, not gradients.

Docking scores are never backpropagated through the autoencoder. Protein information reaches the generator only through which molecules are returned to its training corpus.

Technical details: the ALGen loop and its architecture
ALGen workflow
  • Architecture, stated precisely. The inherited implementation (source) operates on SMILES strings with a deterministic sequence autoencoder. Candidates are produced by Gaussian perturbation around encoded latent points, then decoding, an algorithmic exploration distribution rather than a draw from a learned posterior. Nearby latent points need not decode to valid or similar molecules.
  • Chemical filters (inherited, fixed): QED ≥ 0.5, SA ≤ 3, Tanimoto < 0.5, MW 280.85–700 Da. Novelty is evaluated against the expanding corpus, so “novel” always means relative to an explicit reference population.
  • Fingerprints. Radius-4 Morgan circular fingerprints as sparse unhashed count vectors; similarity is count-vector Tanimoto.
  • Persistent state is the corpus, not the weights. Cb+1 = Cb ∪ Sβ(Pb). Within each inner loop the same pretrained weights are reloaded and fitted to the current corpus; fitted parameters are not carried forward.
  • Where the stochasticity comes from. Each inner loop forms a new train/test partition, and the generation seed is sampled from the temporary test subset, so changing the partition changes the seed molecule and the latent neighbourhood explored. That temporary test subset is part of the generation mechanism, not a held-out validation set. This is why the downstream question in Results cannot be settled from a single run.
  • Relation to prior work. The original CDK2 application was single-target; a later extension handled multi-target affinity across coronavirus proteases. That is a different problem: multi-target affinity rewards favourable binding everywhere, whereas selectivity requires favourable target binding together with deliberately unfavourable antitarget binding.

What the score rewards

J = CDK2 potency − βAT · antitarget liability − promiscuity risk + size control

Four terms, each doing one job. Every input is a receptor-normalised z-score, so the terms are on a common scale (see putting every receptor on one scale).

The four terms of the selectivity score J and what each one is for.
TermWhat it does
+ target Rewards stronger predicted CDK2 binding, capped.
− antitarget Penalises predicted binding at the three scored receptors.
− promiscuity Penalises chemistry associated with broad, non-specific binding.
+ size control Stops the score being won by simply shrinking the molecule.
Technical details: the four terms of the selectivity score J
  • Design constraints J must satisfy. Increase when predicted CDK2 binding strengthens at fixed antitarget binding; increase when predicted antitarget binding weakens at fixed CDK2 binding; decrease when binding weakens uniformly across all receptors. The third is what the gate and the hinge together enforce.
  • Target. JCDK2 = min(zCDK2, zmax), zmax = 3. The cap stops an extreme target score compensating without limit.
  • Antitarget. A reliability-weighted hinge, JAT = Σk∈A wk · max(zk, 0) / Σk∈A wk. The hinge penalises only antitarget scores stronger than that receptor’s own reference level; weaker scores earn no extra reward. Normalising by Σwk keeps the term on a comparable scale.
  • Promiscuity. Four descriptors (aromatic rings, cLogP, HBD, TPSA) with signs fixed a priori from their relationship with measured selectivity (+1 for aromatic rings and cLogP, −1 for HBD and TPSA). Jrisk = Σt max(εt zt, −c), c = 0.75. Each term is floored so no single favourable descriptor gives an unbounded offset to the docking terms, while unfavourable values stay unbounded. The constants are frozen in the deployed objective file and computed over the 2,514 calibration compounds with a complete receptor profile (111 S1, 492 S2, 1,911 S5), not over generated molecules: aromatic rings μ 2.876 σ 1.039 · cLogP μ 3.338 σ 1.287 · HBD μ 2.206 σ 0.988 · TPSA μ 98.272 σ 27.387.
  • Size. Jsize = −[max(nlo − nH, 0) + max(nH − nhi, 0)] / λ, window [20, 34], λ = 5. Zero inside the window; the upper bound discourages size-driven score inflation, the lower bound stops trivially small molecules being favoured.

The CDK2 gate

First require sCDK2 ≤ −7.5. Then rank by J.

Antitarget avoidance cannot rescue a molecule that is not already a sufficiently strong predicted CDK2 binder. Without this gate, the cheapest way to look selective is to bind nothing.

Why this decision?: the two docking thresholds

Two thresholds with different jobs, easy to confuse. −7.0 is a compute floor: only molecules below it proceed to antitarget docking at all, a budget decision limiting how many molecules need ten-grid antitarget docking, with no bearing on eligibility. −7.5 is the eligibility gate: only molecules below it receive J and enter the ranking.

  • The gate is the only one of the four that changes which molecules the objective can see, and the only one with a genuine trade-off: loosening it raises retained-set selectivity but weakens the ability to demote a molecule that has been weakened at every receptor; tightening below −8.0 reduces retained-set selectivity monotonically until the bootstrap interval crosses zero at −8.5.
  • Removing the size window entirely changes 0.2 % of the selected set. It is a bound imposed on the search, justified a priori because an unbounded preference for smaller molecules optimises at a single atom.
  • Stated honestly: because the same reference panel informed these choices, benchmark performance at the deployed values is not independent validation of them. Calibration establishes an operating regime; the controlled comparison in Results tests the deployed objective against an ablated variant of itself, which only requires that the rule was fixed in advance.

Method: the comparison

⚖️ The experiment

Everything in the two sections above exists so that one coefficient could be switched off. The experiment is two complete runs of the same loop that differ in that coefficient and in nothing else.

Two runs, one difference

Two complete ALGen runs were executed, differing only in βAT, the weight on the antitarget term inside the selection score J. Same pretrained model, same 1,060-molecule starting corpus, same schedule, same selection budget, same docking calculations.

βAT = 1 · AT-guided βAT = 0 · AT-ablated

Named the same way throughout the site and on the printed poster: teal is the AT-guided arm, terracotta the AT-ablated one.

Technical details: the two runs and the one difference between them

Nine outer blocks per run. Within a block, each inner loop refits the molecular model to the current corpus, generates proposals and filters them; the corpus is cumulative and also serves as the novelty reference, so “novel” is always relative to an explicit population.

Compute. Batched autoregressive decoding cut the mean generation time for 5,000 proposals from approximately 1,533 s to 9.61 s per inner loop, a factor of about 160. Tanimoto filtering then became the bottleneck, so the pipeline was reordered (MW, QED and SA applied before similarity), accumulated-reference fingerprints were cached, and the remaining comparisons were distributed over 16 CPU workers: approximately 278 s to 14.62 s, a factor of about 19. Specific-set training took 45.82 s per loop. Production 10-loop blocks ran in 12.36 min on average, 9.5–14.5 min observed; before batching, generation alone would have needed roughly 17 h over 40 inner loops.

Hardware: one NVIDIA H100 on an Intel Xeon Platinum 8460Y+ node; LigPrep and Glide on Xeon Platinum 8160, 48 cores per job.

These changes affected throughput only. The objective, the proposal count, the filter thresholds and the selection logic are unchanged.

The four populations

Each block produces four distinct sets of molecules, and almost every number reported later belongs to exactly one of them. Three sit on the main path; the fourth is deliberately taken off it.

Why the monitor sample exists

Every other population is conditioned on docking success. The monitor sample is the only unconditioned readout of what the generator is producing.

Protocol and validation: the four populations
  • The monitor sample is drawn from the filtered set before the −7.0 compute floor, and docked against the complete panel regardless of CDK2 score. The two thresholds do different jobs: −7.0 decides which molecules are docked against the antitarget panel at all, a budget decision; −7.5 decides which molecules are eligible for ranking.
  • Budget 1,500 molecules in block 1 and 500 per later block, drawn across the block’s inner loops rather than in a single draw, so a block’s sample aggregates several independently sampled latent neighbourhoods rather than one.
  • Realised counts: 1,489 in block 1 and 495–500 per block thereafter, in both arms; 5,478 molecules per arm in total.

Why block 1 is the controlled comparison

Only block 1 supports a causal contrast. From block 2 onward the two runs train on different accumulated corpora and diverge.

A reporting artefact, found and corrected. The archived parent record is the prepared state with the highest J under that run’s own scoring configuration. State choice alone can therefore create an apparent cross-run difference even when the chemistry and the docking calculations are shared: −0.025 z for the worst antitarget z in the monitor sample, and −0.011 z in the eligible pool.

A run-independent reduction over prepared states shared by both runs removes that difference to numerical precision, and that reduction is what the controlled comparison uses. The uncorrected figure is retained only as an empirical reporting floor for the downstream question in how far the preference reaches.

A frozen-generator, random-feedback or shuffled-feedback control, replicated across independent stochastic runs, would be needed for the broader “feedback versus no feedback” claim.

What was found

📊 Results

Two complete runs of the same generative workflow, differing in one coefficient: whether the selection score carries an off-target penalty at all. Switching that coefficient off changes which molecules the workflow keeps. In the single block where both runs are offered exactly the same candidates, 13.95 % of the selected molecules differ, and all eight evaluated antitarget kinases move toward weaker predicted binding, at a small cost in predicted CDK2 potency.

Four questions, in order. Does the antitarget term change selection at all? What is selection actually doing inside each pool? What does the change cost in predicted potency? And how far does the preference reach into what the generator produces later?

The five words this section leans on, in case you came straight here from the top. Docking places a molecule in a protein pocket in silico and scores the fit; every number here is predicted, never measured. z is a docking score expressed against that receptor's own baseline, so one receptor's scores can be compared with another's (how). βAT is the one coefficient the two runs differ in: the weight on the antitarget penalty inside the selection score J. AT-guided is βAT = 1, AT-ablated is βAT = 0 (what J rewards). Scored receptors enter that penalty; held-out receptors are measured but never optimised against (why the split).

Changing only the antitarget term changes selection

This is the headline, and it is the one comparison the design supports causally. In block 1 both runs see an identical candidate pool: the same molecules, the same docking calculations. 26,062 molecules satisfy the common eligibility criterion; each selector keeps 2,000. They agree on 1,721, and each keeps 279 the other does not. That is a Jaccard overlap of 0.755, or equivalently: changing only βAT changed 13.95 % of the selected identities.

Swapping 14 % of the set is enough to move the whole selection. Comparing the two 2,000-molecule selected sets, the median receptor-normalised z shifts at all eight antitarget receptors in the direction the objective was built to produce, and at CDK2 in the opposite direction, which is the cost.

How to read Δz

Δz = AT-guided − AT-ablated. For an antitarget, negative means weaker predicted binding, the desired direction. For CDK2, negative is the cost. All eight antitarget 95 % intervals exclude zero.

The five held-out receptors were never in the objective. They move anyway, which suggests the effect is not confined to the three receptors being optimised. They are still evaluated inside the same Glide-based framework, though, so this is corroboration rather than independent confirmation.

Technical details: reward-decile response

Reward-decile response: what a higher reward actually buys

Eligible molecules from all nine blocks were pooled within each run, ranked by that run's own reward, and split into ten equal deciles (n = 74,649 AT-guided; n = 83,224 AT-ablated).

  • With the antitarget term, a higher reward means stronger predicted CDK2 binding and weaker predicted binding at all three scored receptors, with the same pattern more weakly across the held-out set.
  • Without it, a higher reward means broadly stronger predicted binding everywhere: a potent profile rather than a selective one.

Caveat. Because the two configurations differ through βAT, the decile index is a rank within each run, not a common value of J. This is descriptive. It does not replace the block-1 comparison, because the candidate pools differ after block 1.

The potency–selectivity trade-off

No single molecule maximises both predicted potency and predicted selectivity. The non-dominated candidates, those that cannot be improved on one axis without giving up the other, trace a frontier. Among molecules already predicted to bind CDK2 strongly, the AT-guided run reaches larger predicted selectivity margins.

What this does not show: the potency-selectivity trade-off

The honest complication. Across all 10,000 selected molecules per run, the AT-ablated run retains more molecules in the deep CDK2 tail. This is exactly what a narrower objective should do, and it is the strongest thing that can be said against the guided run.

But conditioned on already being in that tail, the AT-guided set has the larger median scored-panel margin in all nine blocks, at similar median CDK2 scores. Applying the random-subset procedure within the restricted tail at τ = −9.5 gives median gains of about +0.22 (Mscored) and +0.17 z (scored-antitarget suppression), favourable in 9/9 blocks. The selectivity gain is not bought by abandoning potency. It is achieved among molecules that are already potent.

Two candidates from the frontier make the shape of the trade-off concrete: 5_1562 combines a CDK2 docking score of −11.78 with Mscored = 3.52; 7_2710 gives up some predicted potency (−10.17) for a larger margin of 4.19.

Scaffold concentration is a real cost. Selection concentrates the population into fewer Bemis–Murcko scaffold families than random selection would (ΔUscaf negative across blocks, in both runs). Selection pressure buys the margin and pays in diversity.

How far does the preference reach?

The selection-level result is solid: the antitarget term demonstrably changes which molecules are kept. The natural next question is a different one: whether that preference survives into the molecules the generator produces later, once the two runs have been training on different corpora for several blocks.

Across blocks 2–9 the mean contrast in the worst scored-antitarget z of the unconditioned monitor samples is −0.124 z, in the expected direction. Correcting for the −0.025 z prepared-state reporting offset measured in block 1, before the runs diverged, leaves −0.099 z. The block-to-block variability along a single trajectory is ± 0.102 z.

The corrected signal is about the same size as the variation already present within one run. With one stochastic trajectory per condition, between-run variability cannot be estimated at all, so the honest statement is that the experiment does not resolve the question, not that the answer is negative.

Why more blocks would not fix this

Blocks 2–9 are successive states of one stochastic process, not independent replicates. An early difference in generated or selected chemistry changes the corpus from which all later models are fitted, so the trajectory is path-dependent. The ± 0.102 z figure is an empirical measure of variation along a completed trajectory, not a standard error, not a confidence interval, and dividing it by √8 would not be meaningful.

Only independent runs from different random seeds can separate a reproducible βAT effect from ordinary trajectory-to-trajectory variation. That is a defined, affordable next experiment, and it is the first item under next steps.

See the numbers: how far the preference reaches
  • Direction, and the estimator it depends on. The headline −0.124 / −0.099 z is a mean, and the contrast is sensitive to that choice: the mean carries the expected sign in 8 of 8 blocks, the median in only 6 of 8 (P = 0.29, two-sided sign test). Consistent with a real but small effect, and equally consistent with drift.
  • Attenuation has a plausible mechanism, not identifiable here. The corpus grows cumulatively, so the molecules contributed by any one selection block become a progressively smaller fraction of later training populations. That is consistent with what is observed; it is not demonstrated by it.

Extreme-tail generation

In the unconditioned monitor sample over blocks 2–9 (3,989 against 3,990 records), the first two thresholds do not resolve a difference: at ≤ −9.0, 4.26 % against 4.91 %, RR 1.15 [0.94, 1.41]; at ≤ −9.5, 1.30 % against 1.70 %, RR 1.31 [0.91, 1.87]. The apparent separation at ≤ −10.0 (RR 3.12 [1.14, 8.58], a block-clustered interval, not a Katz one: the 7,979 monitor records are clustered in eight outer blocks and one block supplies 10 of the 25 AT-ablated events) rests on 33 events in total, 8 AT-guided against 25 AT-ablated. Removing that single block gives RR 1.87 [0.80, 4.41]. The effect does not survive removal of one block. Not a result.

These intervals describe uncertainty within the observed monitor samples. They do not estimate variability across independent ALGen runs: only one complete trajectory exists per condition.

Why best-score comparisons are not evidence

For N independent draws, P(mini Xi ≤ x) = 1 − [1 − F(x)]N, so larger pools produce more extreme minima even when the underlying distribution is unchanged. Equal-budget resampling places the ≈ 0.14 difference between the two best observed CDK2 scores squarely within matched-budget variation.

The percentages below are therefore a near-complete, not a complete, accounting. What it does not show

🧭 Limits and next steps

Every limit below is already stated where it arises. They are gathered here so the boundary of the claim can be read in one place, each one paired with the experiment that would settle it.

The right-hand column is the “next steps” of the title. It is not a wish list: each entry is the specific measurement or control that would remove that particular limit.

The four that bound the claim most. Four more are listed below.
LimitationWhat would settle it
Glide defines both the selection pressure and the primary evaluation signal, so the improvement is not independent of the model that produced it Orthogonal energetic rescoring, then biochemical assays
One stochastic trajectory per condition; downstream propagation unresolved Independent runs from different random seeds
Both arms receive informative feedback, so the experiment does not isolate feedback vs no feedback Frozen-generator, random-feedback and shuffled-feedback controls, replicated
S1 selectivity labels rest on one decisively measured antitarget for 102 of 111 compounds Broader kinome profiling of the reference set

Glide is the docking program that produced every score on this site. S1 is the reference set of 111 compounds measured to be both CDK2-active and selective, defined under the reference chemistry. A stochastic trajectory is one complete run of the generative loop: because each run reshapes the corpus it later trains on, no two runs follow the same path.

What this does not show: four further limitations

Four further limitations, each with what would settle it.

LimitationWhat would settle it
Receptor panel is coarse-grained: retained structures capture experimentally accessible geometries, not their physiological populations Ensemble- or MD-informed receptor weighting
Closely related kinases leave only subtle differences for an empirical scoring function; the three scored receptors are the more sequence-distant of the panel Better scoring, or explicit induced-fit treatment
Selection concentrates the population into fewer scaffold families than random selection Diversity-aware selection terms
Calibration and benchmark used the same reference panel, so benchmark performance is not independent validation of the deployed parameters An untouched external reference set
Where this leaves the claim

None of these are fatal to the claim actually made, that antitarget-aware docking feedback causally changes molecular selection. All of them bound how far that claim can be extended.
